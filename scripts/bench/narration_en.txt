The door creaked open.
"Who's there?" she whispered.
Nobody answered, and the silence felt heavier than any reply.
On the morning of the third of March, the village woke to find the river had turned silver.
Old Tomas had seen many strange things in his eighty-two years, but never anything like this.
He pulled on his boots, grabbed his walking stick, and set off toward the water before anyone else could stop him.
The children followed at a distance, half afraid and half delighted, as children always are when something impossible happens.
"Stay back!" he shouted, though his voice trembled more with wonder than with fear.
By noon, nearly two hundred people had gathered along the bank, each one with a different theory and no proof at all.
Some said it was a curse. Others said it was a gift.
Mira, the baker's daughter, said nothing; she simply knelt, dipped her fingers into the river, and smiled.
The water was warm, warmer than it had any right to be in early spring, and it hummed faintly, like a song remembered from a dream.
That night, for the first time in years, nobody in the village could sleep, and nobody wanted to.
And that, my friends, is how the story of the Silver River began.
